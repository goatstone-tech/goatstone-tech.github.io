What a Certificate of Data Destruction Should Include

Most certificates of data destruction are a logo, a date, and a sentence saying the drives were destroyed. That document will not help you in an audit, and it will not help you after a breach. A certificate is only worth something if it identifies specific devices, names the method used, and records who performed and verified the work.

What the certificate is

It is the per-device record proving a specific storage device was sanitized or destroyed, by a specific method, on a specific date, by a specific person. The federal standard for this is NIST Special Publication 800-88, Guidelines for Media Sanitization. NIST calls the document a Certificate of Sanitization, and provides a sample form organizations can model theirs on. NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 1 was withdrawn on September 26, 2025, and superseded by Rev. 2, published the same day. The sample certificate moved from Appendix G to Appendix C and the fields changed.

The fields NIST's sample certificate records

Person performing the sanitization. Name, title, organization, location, phone, email.

Media information. Vendor or make, media type, model number, serial number, property number, source, classification, and whether the device was operational or damaged.

Sanitization details. Method (Clear, Purge, or Destroy), the specific technique used, the tools used including version number, verification and status, validation, and any notes.

Media destination and disposition. Where the device went: internal reuse, external reuse, recycling facility, manufacturer, or other, with details.

Signature. A signed attestation that the information is accurate, with a date, plus a concurrence section for the person verifying the work.

The four fields that separate a real certificate from a decorative one

1. Serial numbers. "47 hard drives destroyed" is not evidence. If you cannot tie the certificate to the specific devices that left your building, you cannot prove the drive in question was one of them.

2. The specific method. Clear, Purge, and Destroy are not interchangeable, and NIST treats them as distinct categories with different techniques behind each. "Securely destroyed" is marketing language. "Shredded" or "overwritten using a named tool, verified" is a fact.

3. The tool and version. NIST's form asks for the tools used including version. This is what makes a wipe auditable.

4. Verification and a second signature. The form separates the person who performed the sanitization from the person who concurred. One person doing both, with no verification step recorded, is weaker documentation.

Red flags in a certificate

No serial numbers, just a device count. Vague method language with no technique named. No date, or a date that does not match the pickup. No named individual, only a company name. No disposition, so no record of where the material actually went. Issued before the destruction actually happened. Provider still citing NIST 800-88 Rev. 1 as the current standard.

Questions to ask before you hand over the drives

Will the certificate list every serial number? Which sanitization method will you use, and which technique? If drives are wiped, what tool and version, and is verification recorded? Who signs it, and is there a separate verification signature? Will it state the final disposition of the material? How long do you retain your copy of the records? When do I receive it after pickup?

How long to keep it

Keep certificates at least as long as your retention policy for the data that was on the devices, and as long as any regulation covering your industry requires. Store them where you can actually find them. A certificate you cannot locate during an audit is functionally the same as not having one.
